How we pay out production work

A plain-language walkthrough of how Alexandra and Alayna get paid for each item we make — how the split is decided, where you enter it, and how to run a payout. It mirrors the work distribution database in Notion, so the numbers here are the numbers there.

01The core idea

Every item we sell is worth a certain amount of production pay — the labour value baked into its price. We don't track hours. Instead, each item's value is divided into five production stages, and whoever does a stage earns that stage's share.

The five stages

Consult & sketch  ·  Sourcing materials  ·  Cutting & pinning  ·  Sewing  ·  Detailing

How big each stage's slice is depends on the category of the item (a dress is weighted differently than a pair of soakers). Those weights live in the Category Pay Splits table and always add up to 100%. So the whole thing is just:

# For one stage stage pay = Item value × that stage's % × (1 if you did it, 0.5 if split) # Item value comes straight from the sale Item value = Sale price × Units

Add up the stages each person did, and that's what they're owed. Notion's Alexandra owed and Alayna owed formulas do this automatically — you never do the math by hand.

03Tagging who did each stage

On each item in work distribution, each stage has a “by” field with three choices:

ChoiceMeaning
AlexandraAlexandra did this stage — she earns its full share.
AlaynaAlayna did this stage — she earns its full share.
SplitYou did it together — the share is divided 50 / 50.

There are five “by” fields, one per stage: Consult & sketch by, Sourcing materials by, Cutting & pinning fabric by, Sewing by, and Detailing by. Leave a stage's field blank only if that stage genuinely doesn't apply to the category (the split table already zeroes those out).

04Logging an item, step by step

  1. Add a row in work distribution

    Use the matching template (Dress, Soakers, Hair accessories, Hot tools bag) so the checklist and suggested stages come pre-filled. Name the Production item after the design or client.

  2. Set the Category

    Link the Category relation to the right category. This is what pulls in the stage percentages — nothing weights correctly until it's set.

  3. Enter Sale price and Units

    Sale price is the per-item price; Units is how many identical items this row covers. Together they make Item value, the pool being divided.

  4. Tag each stage's “by” field

    For every stage that applies, choose Alexandra, Alayna, or Split. Alexandra owed and Alayna owed update live as you go.

  5. Link the Order (custom work)

    For a custom piece, connect the Order relation so the row carries its Order Stage and stays tied to the customer. Ready-to-wear / shop items can skip this.

  6. Clear “Needs attention”

    The Needs attention formula flags a row that's missing something (no category, no price, an unassigned stage). The Needs stage entries view lists everything still incomplete — aim to keep it empty.

Alexandra took the consult and the sewing (the biggest slice), Alayna took sourcing, cutting, and detailing — and it lands at $250 each. If a stage had been marked Split, its pay would have been halved between the two columns instead.

06Running a payout

When it's time to settle up — say, at the end of a month or when an order is delivered:

  1. Open the Pay review view

    It shows each item with Alexandra owed, Alayna owed, and both Paid checkboxes.

  2. Filter to unpaid & finished work

    Look at rows where Paid Alexandra / Paid Alayna is unchecked and the Order Stage is far enough along that the work is really done. Sum each person's owed column (Notion can total it at the bottom of the view).

  3. Pay each person their total

    Transfer Alexandra her sum and Alayna hers. Pay the two people independently — an item can be part-paid to one and not the other, so the checkboxes are separate on purpose.

  4. Check the boxes to close it out

    Tick Paid Alexandra and/or Paid Alayna on the rows you just settled. Those rows drop out of the unpaid filter, so the next payout only ever shows what's still outstanding.

Rule of thumb Don't mark a stage paid until the work is actually finished and the sale price is final. If a price changes after payout, the owed amounts recompute — reconcile the difference on the next round rather than editing history.

07Edge cases & FAQ

We both worked on one stage

Mark that stage Split. Its pay is divided 50 / 50. (If the real split isn't even, the cleanest fix is to give the stage to whoever did most of it and note the adjustment in Notes, or split the row into two rows.)

A category doesn't use all five stages

That's expected — soakers, bags, and hair accessories have 0% on the stages they don't need, so leaving those “by” fields blank changes nothing. Only the weighted stages pay out.

Multiple identical items

Put the count in Units rather than making a row each — the item value (and every stage share) scales up automatically. Split one row out only if different people did the stages on different copies.

The percentages feel wrong for how the work actually goes

The weights are a policy decision, not a law of nature. If sewing is routinely heavier than 35% for dresses, change it once in Category Pay Splits and it applies going forward. Keep the row at 100%.

Where does the sale price come from?

For custom orders it's the order's agreed price (the same figure the invoice is built from); for shop items it's the listed sale price. Enter the price the customer actually paid for that item, before shipping and tax.
